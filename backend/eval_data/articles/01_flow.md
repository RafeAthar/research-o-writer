## 1. Introduction

Flow is the mental state in which a person performing some activity is fully
immersed in a feeling of energized focus, full involvement, and enjoyment.
Two preconditions are usually emphasized in the literature: a clear goal and
immediate feedback on progress.

## 2. The challenge–skill balance

The single most cited condition for flow is the balance between challenge and
skill. When the challenge of a task substantially exceeds the actor's skill,
the result is anxiety. When the actor's skill substantially exceeds the
challenge, the result is boredom. Flow appears in the narrow band where
challenge and skill rise together and remain roughly matched.

## 3. Disappearance of the self-observer

A frequently reported experiential feature of flow is the temporary
disappearance of the self-observer. People in flow describe losing track of
time and ceasing to monitor themselves from the outside. This is sometimes
described as autotelic absorption: the activity becomes its own reward.

## 4. Conditions that block flow

Three conditions are commonly cited as obstacles to entering flow: ambiguous
goals, delayed or absent feedback, and frequent interruptions that fragment
attention. Removing any one of these is rarely sufficient on its own; the
three tend to need to be addressed together.

## 5. Practical implications

For knowledge workers, the practical implication is that environments which
maximize uninterrupted blocks of time and provide tight feedback loops tend
to produce flow more reliably than environments rich in either freedom or
structure alone.
